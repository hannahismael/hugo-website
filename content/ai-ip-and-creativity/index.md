##### Overview

This strand of my work asks how intellectual property and creative labor should be governed as AI reshapes how creative work is made and valued. Its main vehicle so far has been a series of artist-led participatory assemblies run at Mozilla Foundation, in Los Angeles with the [Berggruen Institute](https://berggruen.org/projects/imaginative-intelligences), and [continuing city by city](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/creative-futures-imaginative-intelligences/).

My interest is a governance one. AI policy debates about creative work are argued largely between rights holders, platforms, and model developers. The people whose labor is actually at stake tend to enter the record late, and usually only through litigation or strike — by which point the terms of the argument are already set. The assemblies were designed to produce a different input: structured, attributable, first-hand evidence from creative workers, in a form that legislatures and multilateral bodies can cite.

I co-edited the resulting synthesis report, *Creativity is Collective: Hollywood's 8 Rules for AI*, authored with 91 assembly participants.

##### Method as a governance instrument

Notice-and-comment and industry consultation reward whoever is already organized enough to file. That skews the evidentiary record toward studios, labels, platforms, and trade associations, and away from freelancers, crew, and platform-native creators — the groups most exposed to displacement, and the least able to absorb it.

The assemblies were built to correct for that selection effect. Participants were sourced through film, TV, and music crew job boards, local creative networks, direct outreach to creators and management firms, university pages, and partner networks, then curated for discipline, seniority, and position in the production pipeline. Compensation and transport were provided, so participating did not require already having slack in your week.

Five half-day assemblies of 12–20 people each ran a consistent structure: grounding, facilitated dialogue on the prompt *"What is creative purpose in the age of AI?"*, blindfolded deep listening to strip out visual hierarchy and status signaling, collective theme selection, then decomposition and physical modeling of that theme. Each of the five landed on its own theme — pain, stream/flow, attention, performance, transmission — tracking the discipline mix in the room.

Everything was transcribed and filmed. The output is an auditable record with a documented sampling frame, which is what made it usable downstream as evidence rather than anecdote.

##### Questions I explored

- How do you design a consultation process whose output holds up as policy evidence rather than sentiment?

- Where does the existing record on AI and creative labor systematically under-represent the people it most affects, and what does that omission do to the rules that get written?

- Participants ranked job displacement well above intellectual property as their central worry, and over 63% of creatives and 75% of technologists did not feel represented by *any* institution — guild and studio included — in the fight for creative rights in tech. If the representation gap is that wide, whose account of creative-sector interests are policymakers actually receiving?

- What do provenance, attribution, and transparency obligations look like when specified by the people downstream of them rather than by model developers?

##### From testimony to policy demands

Nine cross-cutting insights emerged across the assemblies, distilled into **Hollywood's 8 Rules for AI** — framed as design and accountability requirements, not as opposition:

1. **Build for process, not just output** — AI should support exploration rather than shortcut it.
2. **Encourage randomness and glitchiness** — leave room for surprise instead of overdetermining the world.
3. **Keep friction in the creative flow** — moments of constraint and discomfort are generative.
4. **Protect lineage and give credit** — systems should trace their inspirations and acknowledge the histories and cultures behind their data.
5. **Put human purpose at the center** — ask and adapt to creator intent, making purpose the starting point over patterns.
6. **Make AI transparent and conversational** — show how decisions are made and allow real-time intervention.
7. **Value deliberate slowness and depth** — design for layered work instead of forcing instant delivery.
8. **Design for the whole creative ecosystem** — account for labor, culture, and social impact a priori, not as a bolt-on.

Rules 4, 6, and 8 map onto live regulatory questions: training-data provenance and attribution, model transparency and explainability duties, and ex ante impact assessment. The report's organizing claim — *resistance is not rejection, but redesign* — was a deliberate positioning choice. It frames creative-sector input as a design and accountability input to model development rather than a veto on it, which is what allowed the findings to travel into venues where a refusal posture would have been discounted.

##### Policy uptake

**California Senate Judiciary Committee.** *Creativity is Collective: Hollywood's 8 Rules for AI* is cited in the staff background paper for the [joint informational hearing on Artificial Intelligence and Copyright](https://sjud.senate.ca.gov/system/files/2025-12/background-paper-dec-8-stanford-ai-and-copyright-joint-info-hearing_updated.pdf) held 8 December 2025 at Stanford, convened by the Senate Judiciary Committee and the Assembly Privacy and Consumer Protection Committee. The report anchors the paper's section on artists' perspectives, which reproduces the assemblies' finding that creative labor is increasingly precarious and devalued, the representation statistics above, and participant testimony on the collapse of sustainable creative careers.

**UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance.** The work was carried into the multilateral track through the [Public AI Voice and Engagement (PAVE) case book](https://pave.pairs.site/), which documents over 80 participatory and deliberative AI processes and serves as the evidence base for a proposed Citizens' Track on AI governance. That case book fed the dialogue leading up to the first [UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/), convened in Geneva on 6–7 July 2026.

**Participatory AI Research & Practice Symposium.** Findings were presented at [PAIRS](https://pairs.site/), at the 2026 India AI Impact Summit.
